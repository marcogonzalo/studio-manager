Unbilled extras (_change orders_) happen when a client requests changes during a project—upgraded materials, expanded scope, or on-site design tweaks—and the studio carries out the work without updating the budget; Veta helps prevent them by logging each change as an additional budget line with description, phase, and cost, visible both to your team and in the client view.

## What are unbilled extras and change orders in interior design?

In architecture and interior design, a **change order** (or project extra) is any work, product, or service added to or altered from the originally approved scope and budget.

On a live project, changes are normal. Once walls come down, a client decides to add hallway sconces, swap laminate for solid oak, or commission custom joinery that wasn't in the initial brief. Industry benchmarks from construction and renovation management (including _Procore_ and _Construction Industry Institute_ studies on scope creep) consistently rank undocumented scope changes among the top three drivers of net margin erosion in residential projects.

The problem is never that clients ask for changes—the problem is executing them without routing them through the budget first.

## Why do interior design studios lose money on unbilled extras?

For solo practitioners and 1–3 person studios juggling several commissions at once, profit leaks rarely come from a massive miscalculation on day one. They come from the steady drip of small, unrecorded additions:

- **Commercial friction:** halting site momentum over a €180 upgrade feels petty in the moment, yet ten of those across a renovation quietly erase €1,800 of net profit.
- **Gap between site and desk:** a project manager agrees to a tweak on site with the contractor or client, but that conversation never reaches the person maintaining the budget.
- **Late closeout billing:** trying to bill for an extra three months after installation—without having updated the budget when it was requested—triggers client pushback and often ends in an unbilled write-off.

## Why relying on memory, spreadsheets, or WhatsApp fails

When a client texts _"Can we add those wall lights in the hallway after all?"_ and you reply _"Sure, I'll sort it out,"_ you have just accepted a change order with zero budget trail.

Relying on chat threads or standalone spreadsheets breaks down for three reasons:

1. **Messages don't calculate totals:** a voice note or text message confirms intent, but it doesn't recalculate taxes or update outstanding balances.
2. **Spreadsheets drift apart:** if you keep one workbook for internal cost tracking and a separate file for client presentations, every extra means editing two documents without breaking formulas. We cover this in detail in [Veta vs. spreadsheets for interior design budgets](/en/blog/veta-vs-spreadsheets-interior-design-budgets).
3. **Lost context at closeout:** weeks later, nobody remembers whether that hallway change included installation or supply only, or which phase it belonged to.

## How do you log change orders in Veta without losing traceability?

In Veta, you don't spin up a separate "extras spreadsheet." Budget lines and cost control live inside the same project:

1. **Add a new budget line:** open the project's cost control tab and create a line under the right category (Construction, Own fees, External services, or Operations).
2. **Record the description and phase:** write a clear description of what changed (for example, _"Upgrade countertop to quartz per client request"_) and assign the **project phase** where it occurred (Design, Procurement, Construction, Delivery).
3. **Separate estimated vs. actual:** enter the **estimated amount** (`estimated_amount`) to price the change up front. Once the work is executed and billed, fill in the **actual amount** (`actual_amount`) so Veta calculates the variance automatically.

If you are still waiting on a contractor quote, you can mark the line as **"Price TBD"** so the scope change is captured immediately without guessing a number.

## How do you update the budget and control what the client sees?

Not every change on site should be presented the same way to the client. Some are billable extras; others are internal overruns or admin costs your studio chooses to absorb but still needs to measure.

On the [Pro](/en/pro-plan-for-independent-interior-designers) and [Studio](/en/studio-plan-for-architecture-and-interior-design-firms) plans, every budget line lets you control client visibility:

- **Visible to client (open eye icon):** the line is included in the client-facing budget and added to the totals they see.
- **Internal cost (crossed-out eye icon):** only your team sees the line. It counts toward your internal cost and variance tracking, but stays out of the public view and client PDF.

## How do you track payments tied to extras and purchase orders?

Putting a change order into the budget is half the job; the other half is making sure the client pays for it and the supplier gets settled.

In the project's payments tab, you log each transaction by type (**Fees**, **Purchase provision**, **Additional cost**, or **Other**) and link it directly to its source:

- **When the extra is a new product or fixture:** add it to the space, include it in a **purchase order**, and link payments to that PO. Veta shows real-time payment coverage (**Covered**, **Partial**, or **Pending**).
- **When the extra is an out-of-scope service or site expense:** log the payment as an **additional cost** so your team knows whether that extra has been settled or is still pending before closing the books.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is an extra or change order in an interior design project?

Any change requested by the client after approving the initial budget: upgrading a finish, expanding the scope of work, or modifying the design during construction. If it isn't logged and priced separately, the studio absorbs the cost and loses margin.

### Why do interior design extras go unbilled?

Because they are usually agreed upon during site visits, phone calls, or WhatsApp messages and never make it into the budget in real time. Months later at project closeout, those scattered changes are either forgotten or awkward to bill without a prior paper trail.
